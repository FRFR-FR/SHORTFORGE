## Narration script

What if the windows in your house came with a tax bill? In 1696, England introduced a tax linked to the number of windows in a home. More windows could mean a larger bill. Assessors counted the openings, and the number went into the household's assessment. Some owners responded by closing windows to reduce what they owed. Surviving tax records even note openings that had been closed. But a bricked-up window doesn't always prove tax avoidance. Some Georgian houses used blank windows to keep their facades symmetrical. So historians have to distinguish design choices from tax responses. The tax remained for more than 150 years, until it was repealed in 1851. Critics had raised concerns about the effects of dim, poorly ventilated homes. A rule meant to count windows changed how people thought about their walls. Sometimes the biggest effect of a rule is the behaviour it creates.

## Factual guardrails

- The window tax was introduced in England in 1696.
- Assessment depended on the number of windows and was structured in bands; do not imply one universal flat charge per window.
- Some owners did close windows to reduce liability, and surviving tax records include entries recording closures.
- A blocked or blank window is not automatically proof of tax avoidance; symmetry and architectural design can also explain blind windows.
- The tax was repealed in 1851, after criticism that included concerns about light and ventilation.
